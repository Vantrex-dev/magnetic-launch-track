The magnetic launch track

Picture a bullet train that never stops accelerating, running inside a sealed tunnel up a mountain. At the top, it lets go of a rocket already moving at several kilometers per second.

Like a bullet train

Superconducting magnets on the sled induce currents in the track as it moves. Those currents push back and lift the sled, so nothing touches the track at speed.

Like a particle accelerator

Coils along the track switch on in sequence. The sled rides the moving magnetic wave, just as a particle rides the radio-frequency wave in a linac.

Unlike either

It keeps accelerating the whole way. Track length is set by the exit speed and the g-load you allow.

How the wave pulls the sled

Each coil switches on as the sled approaches and off as it passes, so the pull is always forward.

Inside the magnetic coils

The coils turn electricity into forward push. There are two sets of magnets, one riding on the sled and one lining the track, and they push and pull on each other across a small air gap.

Each sled magnet is lined up with a track coil. The coil current is timed so that it attracts the magnet ahead and repels the one behind, so every magnet feels a push forward.

On the sled

Superconducting coils, cooled with liquid helium, carry a steady current and make a strong magnetic field of a few tesla. Once charged, they need almost no power to keep going. The sled itself carries no engine.

Levitation coils

Loops in the track walls are wired so that the moving sled magnet induces currents in them. Those currents push back and lift the sled once it is moving fast enough. At low speed, wheels or air bearings carry the weight.

Propulsion coils

These are driven with current. A controller switches them on as the sled approaches and off as it passes, and only the coils near the sled are powered at any moment. This keeps the electronics and losses manageable.

Frequency f = v ÷ (2 × pole pitch). The power figure is thrust times speed (about 294 kN at 3 g for a 10-tonne sled) and is why the launch needs huge energy storage. Values are illustrative, not a design.

Why the track must be straight

Any bend at speed pushes the sled sideways with an acceleration of v²/r. At 8 km/s, even a gentle curve is punishing, so the track is nearly straight and climbs a mountain along one shallow slope.

How much fuel does the track save?

A rocket must burn fuel to gain speed, and each extra km/s needs far more fuel than the last. This is the rocket equation: Δv = ve × ln(m0 ÷ mf). A track that hands the rocket speed before ignition removes some of that Δv, and the saving compounds.

The hard parts

The air

At 8 km/s at sea level, air pressure on the front of the sled would be around 380 atmospheres. The track runs in a vacuum tunnel, exits high on a mountain, and uses a plasma window, a magnetically confined plasma that holds back air with no solid barrier.

The power

Energy is cheap, about 9 kWh per kilogram at 8 km/s. Delivering it in a few minutes is the problem, and it needs huge capacitor banks or flywheels.

The scale

The physics is straightforward. The infrastructure is not: 100 to 1,000 km of precisely aligned, superconducting track.

The last step

The sled gets the rocket most of the way to orbital speed (7.8 km/s). A small rocket stage then circularizes the orbit.
